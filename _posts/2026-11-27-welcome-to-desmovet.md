Welcome to desmoVet. I guess I should have introduced the platform before my [first article](/articles/calculator-in-your-pocket/) post, but as usual I got carried away and jumped the gun. I have been working on desmoVet for the last few years, building up clinical tools as I see gaps in efficiency and tools that I regularly find myself thinking, "Ugh, I wish there was an easier way to do this." That is kind of how desmoVet originated.

## How it started

It was the second semester of my second year in veterinary school, and we were being lectured by an equine veterinarian on fluid therapy. The professor was explaining fluid rates and additives in foals, and she explained to us how she used a calculator on the website of Cornell's College of Veterinary Medicine to calculate how much 50% dextrose was needed to add into a bag of fluids to make a glucose infusion rate of 4 mg/kg/min, for example. The catch was Cornell's calculator spit out the infusion rate depending on what amount of 50% dextrose you put in. So she explained that she would type numbers into the calculator until she got the rate she was looking for. I remember sitting there in class thinking, well, that seems like a waste of time… I bet I can do better than that.

Previously, I had built Excel calculators for other things like sensitivity and specificity, and other common mathematical calculations that were complicated enough that I was sick and tired of plugging long equations into my calculator. So that's what I did: I opened Excel and I started making a formula and table that allowed me to plug in my target glucose infusion rate, the concentration of dextrose that I had, the size of my fluid bag, and the body weight of the foal, which then spat out the amount in milliliters that you would need to add to your bag of fluids to achieve your desired glucose infusion rate in mg/kg/min. At that point I had saved myself from ever needing to tirelessly plug numbers into Cornell's calculator, though to be fair I had no intention of practicing equine medicine at that time. What's the point of having a calculator that works, solves a problem, and saves time if I am never going to use it?

This was around the time that AI was first starting to come around, and I was known in my group of friends as "the AI guy" (AI meaning artificial intelligence, not artificial insemination, as many veterinarians commonly confuse the acronym). So I went to ChatGPT and I said, take this Excel sheet that I have made a calculator for and write it into code for a website. To my amazement, it spat out code and made the HTML file for my glucose infusion rate calculator website. Having absolutely no background in any sort of technology, I then asked it, "What do I do with this now?" ChatGPT then gave me options on what platforms to upload the HTML file to, and step-by-step guides on how to publish my website. So I spent that night procrastinating from studying for my fluid therapy exam and eventually developed and posted "Kyle's Glucose Calculator." The website had three colors: green, yellow, and white, and it did one thing: it gave you the amount of glucose you needed to add to your fluid bag to achieve your desired rate. At the time I thought it was incredibly cool. I developed an Excel calculator, and with the help of AI, just a few hours later I had my own website up and running.

Long story short, I enthusiastically shared my new website with my professor, and she thought it was the coolest thing ever. We eventually went on to get it published so that other students could use it (although it's hidden so deep somewhere that I don't think anyone has ever used it). Long story short, that's exactly why I continued to develop desmoVet. I wanted tools and calculators that were actually useful to be at my fingertips when I enter practice, and that other people could benefit from. So I took "Kyle's Glucose Calculator," continued to develop my tools in similar fashion, and eventually came up with my hub: desmoVet.

## What's in a name

I went through countless iterations of what I was going to "brand" my calculator hub, because I wanted something that wasn't just a catchy name, but something that had meaning to it, and something that captured the essence of what I wanted my hub to be. Desmosomes are tiny connections that bind adjacent cells together and are found in high concentrations in tissues that endure significant mechanical stress, such as the heart. That was it. Connections at the heart of veterinary medicine. Tools, calculators, and tutoring, built to help when stress is highest and provide a real mechanical solution. desmoVet. That was it.

## What's here

I continued to develop various tools to save myself time and help other veterinary students learn difficult topics. Keeping with the name, I am a firm believer that if you understand the normal basic physiology of animals, you can solve and understand complex diseases and determine treatment based on that. I was a physiology tutor for the underclassmen in my veterinary school for two years, and I was constantly reminded of how understanding the physiology would later benefit you in learning diseases and their treatment. With that, the Cardio Tutor, Neuro Tutor, and Renal Tutor were developed. My hopes and dreams for those platforms are for them to be used as tools to aid in visually understanding and building knowledge, to make future and more complex learning easier.

Another one of my favorite tools I developed is Bloodwork Abnormals. Writing records as a fourth-year veterinary student is hard enough as it is when you are first starting out. Many clinicians and residents wanted us to write out line by line which bloodwork parameters were abnormal in the medical record. Listing out the abnormal parameters and including appropriate units got old real fast, especially in patients with dozens of abnormal parameters who were getting bloodwork done multiple times a day. So, like my other tools, I developed Bloodwork Abnormals so that I could upload the bloodwork results and the tool would spit out the abnormals, list them, and appropriately format them so they were copy-and-paste ready for my medical record. What a time saver, if you ask me.

Great, so far these tools have mostly benefited veterinary students, but what about practicing veterinarians? That is really where my Small Animal Anesthesia Calculator comes in handy (don't worry, there is also an equine drug calculator, EquiCalc). What I wanted to do with this set of calculators is reduce medical errors. One thing that greatly sets apart human and veterinary medicine is the delivery of medical orders. The first practice that I worked in was a small mom-and-pop clinic with just a couple of veterinarians and a couple of technicians. Before doing anesthesia, like in many small privately owned clinics, the doctor would tell the technician what dosing she wanted to give the animal for pre-medications and induction. Now, I am the type of person that will immediately forget something as soon as it is told to me, so I was always amazed at how the technicians remembered what dosing the doctor wanted. While this might be a common practice in veterinary medicine, medication orders in human hospitals are written or entered electronically, and verbal orders are the exception, for good reason: to avoid mistakes. Avoid the "Was it one or two mg per kg that she asked for?" I know there are tools out there (mostly paid subscriptions), and I am sure there are clinics that have their own Excel calculators just for this. I wanted to make my own, though. So I made an anesthesia protocol builder so that you can plug in your patient's information and add medications for pre-med, induction, and CRIs. The best part? You can print out the order and hand it to your technician. The doctor still checks every dose before it goes out, but now it's in writing. Problem solved. Written orders to reduce medical errors, make your life and your technician's life easier, and, most important of all, improve patient care.

## Why

That's really the bottom line. Improving patient care. Whether it be through enhancing veterinary students' understanding of normal physiology or easy access to protocol builders that reduce medical errors, that's why I developed desmoVet. I hope you find the beauty in its simplicity and use it to make your life easier and more efficient, and to enhance your patients' care.
