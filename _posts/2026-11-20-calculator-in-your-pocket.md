## The calculator

"You won't always have a calculator in your pocket." I heard my middle school teachers say it every time my classmates complained about learning long division by hand. Why couldn't we just pull out a calculator and find out what 89 divided by 8 was? Divide by 8, carry the 1... Why spend all that time working out the decimals by hand when you could type a few numbers and get your answer?

Flash forward to today. Everyone has a calculator in their pocket, so it looks like my seventh grade math teacher was wrong. And I wouldn't be ashamed to show them how many times I've used mine to add 23 to 88.

Was my teacher wrong that I wouldn't always have a calculator in my pocket? Yes. But was that ever really the point? Probably not. Long division was about building problem-solving skills and neural connections, the kind that eventually led me to become a doctor, not a mathematician.

## Why it matters now

Why is this more relevant than ever? AI. Whether you like it or not, artificial intelligence is here to change the world, everything we do, everything as we know it. Some people ask, "Is AI really going to change our everyday lives, or is it just a trend that will come and go?" No one really knows where AI is going. But I know where it's not going: away.

I also grew up when not every household had a computer. You went to the library to use one, or you had a desktop at work that stayed at work. That was my teachers' argument for why we had to handwrite our essays, and I'd bet schoolteachers made the same argument when typewriters first showed up. But was handwriting essays really about whether I'd have a computer? No. Again, it was about building skills, and ultimately improving my chicken-scratch handwriting.

The same story is true for AI. You can use it to solve for X in math problems you couldn't even type into a calculator. You can have it write an entire essay for you without thinking about it or pressing more than a couple dozen keys. Heck, you don't even need a keyboard; you can just talk to it. AI is here to make our lives easier. It saves us time. It saves us energy. It saves us brain space.

Veterinary medicine is no different. There's an argument to be made, just like my schoolteachers made, that writing your own medical records, instead of letting AI type them from a recording of your conversation with a client, is a skill every competent clinician must have. They're not wrong.

## The milking parlor

I asked myself a version of that question, and asked the resident standing next to me, after palpating hundreds of dairy cows as they rode out of the never-ending merry-go-round of a milking parlor. It sure looked easy for the clinician running the herd check to reach in, never even look down, and call each pregnancy through her ultrasound goggles. So why did we have to palpate every single cow by hand? In practice we'll surely have an ultrasound too. Isn't it better medicine, and better for students, to learn the more accurate and precise tool instead of the caveman medicine of palpating every cow?

Well, yes and no. Palpating by hand teaches you what a pregnancy actually feels like, how the uterus sits, and what normal is. That's what lets you notice when the ultrasound image doesn't add up, and it's what lets you keep working when the machine dies in a muddy pen at 2 a.m. That's the core argument: learn the foundation first, then use the tool. Palpation is the foundation. The ultrasound is the tool.

Medical records work the same way. Writing a good record is a skill every decent veterinarian needs, because it's how you learn what belongs in one. Once you know that, you can tell when an AI-written record is wrong. Learn to write records first. Then use AI to write them faster.

## What AI actually does

What does AI do, then? Above all, it saves time. Sure, there's a learning curve, but that comes with any new piece of equipment. Once you can write a good record yourself, the time you spend learning your tools, which are evolving at lightning speed, is time spent becoming a better doctor.

Now I'm going to make a claim that may be controversial — using AI makes you a better doctor.

See what I did there? For those of you not yet deeply entangled with AI, the hallmark of AI-generated writing is the overuse of em dashes. You can spot it a mile away. Now go look through your email replies and count how many times someone clearly pasted your email into AI and had it write back for them. Case in point!

How much time would you save every day if you didn't have to write emails full of formalities and pleasantries? Does typing emails keep your brain sharp and your writing skills exercised? Sure. But here's what I'm going to argue: AI saves you time you can give back to your patients, AND it keeps your critical thinking skills even more exercised.

How could that be possible?

## The exam room

You save time writing medical records, and the dozens of times a day you'd write out how a client should give Fluffy probiotics for the next week to help with the diarrhea. Instead, you get to have a more thoughtful, engaged, personal conversation with Fluffy's owner. You can talk about how her son made the college baseball team instead of scribbling down that Fluffy eats Blue Buffalo chicken and rice, so you don't have to wonder later: was Fluffy on Blue Buffalo, or was that my last patient? You don't have to remember, because AI recorded, transcribed, and typed it into the record for you.

Then you step out of the room, click a few buttons, and generate discharge instructions, so Mrs. Fonte doesn't call the clinic at 4:58 because her husband can't remember when to give Fluffy the probiotic or what the smelly paste in the pink box is for. You saved time, and your client feels like she talked with a human who cared about her pet and her family, not a robot who hands out probiotics and sends pets and owners on their way. You can even have AI note that her son made the college baseball team, so you can ask about it next time you see Fluffy.

That's what makes you a good doctor.

## STAT!

A dog who jumped through a window after a squirrel comes in bleeding profusely. True story. He arrives just as Mrs. Elmer walks in with her 18-year-old cat with chronic kidney disease and every other feline disease known to mankind. Stressful? Definitely. Especially because Mrs. Elmer is your colleague's client. Your colleague is on vacation in the Bahamas, and you agreed to squeeze her in because she said her kitty needed to be seen today. Of course you said yes, because... you're a vet.

Now you have a STAT and Mrs. Elmer's kitty, whose medical record is 567 pages long, and you haven't even peeked at it to learn the cat's name, let alone his history.

AI to the rescue! In 17 seconds it summarizes everything about Mrs. Elmer's kitty, in chronological order, on a single page you can read as you walk to the exam room. Oh, and his name... Sweetums. *His* name. Sweetums. *His* name. Sweetums. You repeat it to yourself on the way in, because you wouldn't dare offend Mrs. Elmer by misgendering her beloved 18-year-old tomcat... what was it? Ah. Sweetums.

The summary helped. But you still want to spend an hour writing his record by hand, plus the 14 others you fell behind on today? Sure. Do it. You are, after all, a vet. Fifteen records, no problem, easy work. You glance at the clock: 5:51 p.m. You were supposed to leave at 5. Ha! That's funny. Don't you remember? You're a vet.

## Where it falls short

Okay, really, all jokes aside. Those records can be done with a click. The AI fills in the template you uploaded and writes in your exact style, because a few minutes of setup taught it how you write. Nifty.

You'd be right to say you're far from done, though. You still have to proofread and edit every record it wrote. And this is where the milking parlor comes back. AI makes mistakes, and it makes them confidently. That 17-second summary of Sweetums' 567 pages is only as good as what it caught. If it missed a drug reaction buried on page 312, or a note about which medications his kidneys can't handle, you need to be the one who notices. The only reason you can catch that is because you learned to read histories and write records yourself first. And before you record a single appointment, get your client's okay, and know where that recording goes.

Even with proofreading, you're a heck of a lot closer to done. By the time you'd have finished typing your first history, AI has drafted all fifteen.

## The point

Back to Sweetums. He's a stumper. And at the end of the day, you want to get out of there and watch Game of Thrones. So far, AI has made you more personable and likeable to your clients, and it's saved you time on records. But it hasn't made you a better doctor or helped you practice better medicine.

Ugh, Mrs. Elmer's kitty... what was it? Sweetums!

Here's where it makes you a better doctor. All the time you saved not writing about how great it was to see Petunia and what a sweet German Shepherd she is, you spend thinking about the complicated kitty. You have time to sit, to think, and to research the best, most up-to-date therapies, so Mrs. Elmer can have her Sweetums for years to come.

That's what makes you a better doctor.

Using AI doesn't make you lazy. It doesn't make you a bad doctor. You don't lose the ability to work up complex cases or write medical records because AI does some of that for you, as long as you learned to do it yourself first. It makes you a better doctor because it saves you time. It saves your brain's glucose for treatment options instead of trying to remember, at the end of the day, what diet Fluffy was on. You get more time for what being a doctor actually is: building real relationships with your clients, practicing top-tier medicine because you had time to think, and giving Sweetums some extra loving.

Will AI replace veterinarians? I don't know. Nobody does. I know you're skeptical. You're a vet, after all, and there aren't eight research papers to back me up yet. But I know where AI is not going: away.

So learn the foundation, then use the tool. Your teachers were right about the long division. They were just wrong about the calculator.
